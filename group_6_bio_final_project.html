Research Question

Our mutant lacked Nsp 4 and 6 on the sides, and was just NSP 5 alone. Does the mutant cause variations in NSP 5’s effects on yeast cells?

Our growth assay showed that WT and Mut grew about the same: little without Nirmatrelvir and more with it. C145A exhibited around the same level of growth at 40h both with and without nirmatrelvir; this was expected.

2

Background and hypothesis

  • RNA virus uses host ribosomes to make two polyproteins, which are chains of proteins covalently attached to each other. The proteins in these chains are non-structural. The first polyprotein has 10 NSPs, the second has 16, which include the first 10 plus six others. NSP 5 (main protease or Mpro) is a protease that cleaves the covalent bonds between itself and NSP 4 and 6, then it goes on to separate many of the other NSPs from each other. NSP 4 and 6, once cleaved, make double membrane vesicles using parts of the endoplasmic reticulum. Replication can occur without interference inside these vesicles.
  • After cleaving the polyprotease, Mpro can still cause harm to the cell; and there are two ways this might happen. It may cleave the yeast proteins by its normal catalytic activity, or it may render the yeast proteins inactive simply through interacting with them. To test whether Mpro is catalytically active, it can be grown in two different cultures: one with only NSP 5 and another with NSP 5 attached to NSPs 4 and 6. If catalytically active, NSP 5 will cut itself out from the other two proteins and make a stain on the Western blot assay identical to that of the Mpro expressed alone.
  • Our mutant was Mpro without NSPs 4 and 6. We predict that it will behave like the wild type. The nirmatrelvir will deactivate Mpro, causing the yeast to grow uninhibited.
3

Experimental Design

  • We used a P416Gal plasmid. The plasmid contained seven important DNA sequences: CEN and ORI sequence for replication start in bacteria, ARS for replication in yeast, AMP-resistance for bacteria identification, Ura-3 for identification in yeast, GAL, a promoter induced by galactose which controls Mpro.
  • Two growth assays were performed. The first was of two cell cultures both with Mpro. One culture had galactose and the other raffinose. Raffinose acted as a control to see if Mpro was expressed in the GAL culture. This is because raffinose should not have activated the gal promoter and Mrpo growth. Three different variants of Mpro were grown, with C145A being another control. C145A is a mutant that disables catalytic activity in Mpro. Both graphs are shown below.
[Image Placeholder]
  • In our second growth assay, we had three different strains of Mpro: a wild type strain (WT); C145A mutant. and a mutant (MUT) lacking NSP 4 and 6. The plasmid can be transformed, or put, into a yeast or bacteria cell. The identifying markers allow us to confirm if the cells have the plasmid. Yeast can be grown in synthetic media which lacks uracil; similarly, bacteria can be grown in media with ampicillin. Only the successful transformants will survive the media.
  • We grew three different copies of each of the three strains in one cell dish, and made two dishes; each dish had a different media. One dish had galactose present, the other had Nirmatrelvir. Nirmatrelvir is an inhibitor of Mpro which covalently bonds to it.
  • The cultures were incubated at ~30 degrees celsius. At three different times, measurements were taken of the optical density of the total 18 different cultures. Optical density measures how much light can pass through a cell culture: the more cells the less light and therefore more optically dense.
  • C145A is a negative control which allows us to see what happens to cells when Mrpo is deactivated. WT is a positive control in which Mpro functions normally. These allow us to see the unique effects of MUT Mpro on the cell. The data of these effects can be contrasted against the cell with Nirmatrelvir.
